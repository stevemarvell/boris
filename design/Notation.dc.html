Standard genealogical form
What the conventions are, and which of them this data can actually support.
ConventionUsedWhy
Hourglass chart
ancestors above, descendants below one person		 Yes Exactly what the overview is. Worth calling it by its name — it is a form readers of genealogy already know how to scan.
Drop-line descent
vertical line from the marriage line down to children		 Yes The couple unit is a drop-line chart. Unchanged from the standard.
Sibship line
horizontal bracket grouping children of one union		 Yes Adopted, including the name — it was called a “bus” in the first draft, which is networking vocabulary, not genealogy.
Marriage line
horizontal line joining two partners		 Yes Solid only. The standard also has dashed for cohabitation and slashed for divorce; the data records neither, so drawing them would be invention.
Sex on the symbol
standard: male a square, female a circle		 Adapted Drawn as a border colour instead — dark blue and dark pink. Needs sex added to a person; until then it is inferred from first names, which is a guess.

Colour is not a safe sole channel: roughly 1 in 12 men cannot separate these two reliably, which is exactly why the standard uses shape. Shape should follow.
Birth order
siblings run oldest to youngest, left to right		 Cannot No dates are stored. The sibship line currently sorts by name, which looks like birth order to anyone who knows the convention — and is not.

The honest gap
Two of the six conventions need data the app does not hold. Adopting the standard form without them produces a chart that looks authoritative and quietly misleads: a reader fluent in the notation will take left-to-right as birth order.
Either add sex and birth date to a person — a real change to the model, which currently stores a name, parents and a spouse — or say plainly in the interface that siblings are in name order. Drawing it and hoping nobody reads it as birth order is the one option that is not available.
